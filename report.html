Introduction

Bird habitats influence how birds move, forage, and fly, which can lead to differences in their physical traits. We wanted to explore whether habitat type affects key characteristics, with a particular focus on body mass. We found a paper that suggest that there is a link between body mass and habitat structure within Passeriformes, in particular when comparing dense and open habitats. This motivated us to test whether the AVONET data set shows similar patterns. Our study focuses on Passeriformes, the largest and most diverse bird order, representing around 60% of all bird species. Based on this, we asked:
###What is the relationship between body mass and open vs. dense habitats in Passeriformes, and do other traits help explain habitat density?


Data Overview

For this rerport, we will be using the data set AVONET: morphological, ecological and geographical data for all birds. Each row is a species, containing information about its functional traits including ecological variables, continuous morphological traits, and information on range size and location. We chose to focus on Habitat, Habitat density, Mass and some other body measurements.

First we were curious to see which type of habitats were most common in each group of dense and open habitats.


We found that Forest were most common within dense habitats, and open habitats being wider spread but with the majority belonging to shrubland, forest and woodland habitats. Beacause of the strong correalation between forest and dense habitats, we decided not to include habitat as a exploratory variable in our modelling.

Predictive Modelling

Choice of Model

We wanted to see if mass as the explanatory variable alone is enough to predict whether a species is from a dense habitat or not, or if adding several other body measurements would create a better model. So then we created two models using logistic regression, as our outcome is binary (Dense habitat/Open habitat).


Compearing Two Models

Splitting the data, we used 75% of the data set as our training data and then 25% as our test data. Furthermore, we used cross validation and split the data into ten folds to check the generalizebility of the model and avoid overfitting.

We created a workflow, using only mass as the explanatory variable.

Then we created a second model using several other body measurements as explanatory variables, including: Mass, wing length, beak length, width, depth, wing length and tail length. Then we fittede the model.

Then we compered the two models, based on area under the ROC curve.

Looking further into the second model, we see that some explanatory variables, in particular Beak.Depth and Beak.Width seem to have a correlation with the type of habitat of a species. The positive coefficient in Beak.Depth tells us that there is a positive correlation, indicating that a larger value in Beak.Depth gives us a larger probability of the species being from a dense habitat. While the negative coefficient in Beak.Width tells us that there is a negative correlation, with a larger value decreasing the probability of the species being from a dense habitat. Looking at the extremely low p.values tells us that the observed difference is unlikely to be due to chance. Furthermore, overall the statistical errors seem low, suggesting that model is confident in that the coefficients are estimated with little variability.

Limitations

One limitation of our project is that the trait values in the data set are based on species averages, even though the number of samples per species varies widely (from 1 to 784 individuals). This may cause some of the values to be skewed due to outliers and extreme values. Another limitation is that when we were doing the modeling we discovered that the number of data for when habitat density is 1 is almost twice as much as that of 2 and 3. So in order to make a more even sample size, we combined habitat density 2 and 3 together and considered it as “Open” while 1 is considered “Dense”, this may cause some problems as only half the data for open density habitats are actually for open density while the other half are technically considered semi-open.


Summary

In conclusion, our results differed from the journal we compared against, which reported that birds in dense habitats tend to be lighter than those in open habitats. This difference may be due to the scope of the data: the journal focused on 55 species in central Spain, while we analysed all Passeriformes species available in the AVONET dataset, along with the limitations of our study. In our analysis, we found no clear relationship between body mass and habitat density. However, some other traits—especially beak depth and beak width—showed stronger associations with habitat type. Future work could explore these traits further to better understand how they relate to habitat use.
